Auditing Not-Knowing:The Universal Ignorance Audit and the Epistemology of an AI-Assisted Research Pipeline

Rowan Brad Quni-Gudzinas QNFO, independent research, Amsterdam, the Netherlands  ·  rowan.quni@qnfo.org  ·  ORCID 0009-0002-4317-5604
Sciforum-210010Poster

1Introduction

Ignorance is usually handled as an absence: list the gaps, then fill them. This work treats not-knowing as a structure with parts that can be named: load-bearing assumptions (scaffolds), models mistaken for the world (map–territory errors), felt anomalies (wobbles), defended zones (protected ignorance) and productive capacities (actionable and relational ignorance).

It builds on agnotology [3], specified ignorance [2], ignorance as a driver of science [1], judgment under uncertainty [4], forecaster calibration [5] and intellectual humility [6]. AI-assisted research makes fluent text cheap and moves the bottleneck to evaluation: a pipeline must be able to say what it does not know about its own outputs.

Objectives

  1. Present the Universal Ignorance Audit (UIA) [7], a fifteen-question, five-phase instrument for making not-knowing legible in any domain.
  2. Read one documented case through it: a published paper from the author’s AI-assisted pipeline [8], judged machine-generated by two AI-assisted forensic analyses that made errors of their own.
  3. Propose governance principles and state testable claims.

Administration protocol

  1. Name the target X before questioning.
  2. Answer every question; if one seems inapplicable, say why and stretch to find its relevance.
  3. Write the answers down.
  4. Do not resolve during Phases 1–4.
  5. Allow silence after Question 14.
  6. Question 15’s answer seeds the next pass.

Case analysis and implementation

The findings of two separate AI-assisted forensic analyses (assessments of whether the paper was machine-generated) were re-coded post hoc, by the author, into UIA categories; the analyses were then read with the same categories. Software: ignorance-audit 0.1.0 (Python) checks R1 and R2, chains passes from Q15 (R6), runs the audit with any text model and scores the 2028 error-category claim; 20/20 tests pass (19 Oct 2026).

3Case and interpretation

3.1 The case, re-coded in UIA categories

Q1 ScaffoldInline meta-tags labelling the text’s own modes; citation keys that resolve to nothing: the production process left visible.

3.2 The pipeline’s response (self-reported)

  • Disclosure of AI involvement, with corrections, instead of deletion.
  • A generation-specific gate for energy-budget errors, unresolvable citations, scaffold overload and self-referential metrics.
  • Adversarial validation over self-review.
  • Auditing the auditors with Q1, Q2 and Q10 [8].

3.4 The meta-audit (AI-drafted) bounds the method

  • Assumes ignorance is mainly generative: risky in clinical or safety-critical use.
  • Listing a blind spot is not seeing it.
  • Analytic and extractive; underweights receptive, non-verbal knowing.
  • Favours articulate, time-rich users. Sibling questions followed; several entered the final form.

Limitations

  • One case, coded post hoc by a party to it: illustrative, not validating.
  • The instrument is unvalidated and auditor-dependent by design.
  • Not culture-free; no substitute for domain expertise.
  • Claims sit in a preprint, not a registry, in the register format the analyses flagged as an AI marker.

5Conclusion

We propose that, in an AI-assisted pipeline, epistemic legibility is the core governance problem, and that its failures are recursive: the analyses that caught the paper’s scaffolds made unverified claims of their own. Verifiers need auditing too.

  • Audit before assertingAudit major claims before publishing.
  • Disclose, don’t concealConcealment makes quality an integrity issue.
  • Provenance as a gate“How was this produced?” is a required field.
  • Gate for generationTest fluent models’ own failure modes.
  • Invite adversariesPublish disconfirmation conditions.
  • Audit the auditorsEvery verification layer is another map.
